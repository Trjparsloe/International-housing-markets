JAPAN

It's a standard rite of passage for anyone interested in the built environment to venture to Japan at some point.

Housing is as much of a product of socio-cultural factors as it is of political and economic ones, and from both an architectural and housing market perspective Japan is utterly fascinating. It is a country where 'temporary' buildings are viewed as a standard part of the urban landscape. Architects and developers tear down buildings on average every 30 years. This in turn means that land values remain relatively high, but buildings themselves depreciate rapidly.

This constant rebuilding obviously doesn't seem great from an ESG angle, and it will be interesting to see how that might change as the sustainability agenda gains momentum. But what it does do is enable an incredible freedom of experimentation in terms of architectural typologies and construction methods.

It also makes it an absolute dream for experimental architects, where there aren't the same planning laws that require new development to be in keeping with a prescriptive local aesthetic. There are no strict design guidelines or conservation areas in the same way that there are in Europe. The planning system instead prioritises functionality and safety over form.

The other striking feature is the efficiency of space. While the house itself depreciates, the land certainly does not. High inheritance taxes mean that sections of land must often be sold off or subdivided to meet such obligations, creating incredibly tight plots. In Tokyo, a 50 square meter plot is not uncommon.

There are many other factors that make its housing culture particular, like its sunshine laws and the classic two year lease that dominates the rental market. As the country increasingly urbanises, housing density continues to increase, and the demand for novel solutions in spatial design only becomes more urgent.
